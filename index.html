Arduino + Processing, webcam — interactive device — 2nd year

Taking the Game Boy Camera as a direct reference point, this project asks what it means to design a system where limitation and imperfection aren't obstacles to agency and creativity, but the very things that make them possible.

This project sits in the tension between the control and the freedom a device offers its user. A system can hand its user agency and still leave them choosing between a small set of simple options — and that's exactly what makes you curious about where the real limits of that agency sit. Freedom is never absolute. Creativity is what happens inside the space that's left.

Control comes out of a nostalgia for 8-bit consoles, the Game Boy in particular. Consoles, and the small ecosystem of accessories built around them, offer an imperfect but compelling model — one whose appeal has nothing to do with speed, polish, or precision.

A webcam captures the user's surroundings and shows the result live on a screen, while potentiometers and buttons let them alter color, contrast, brightness, and switch between pixelating the image or turning it into halftone. Turning the dials and pressing the buttons makes the interaction feel different from how we're used to capturing our surroundings today, but familiar from how we used to do it a few decades back.

Analog photography, AI, Processing, CRT monitor, negatives — installation — 4th year

This project started by exploring comfort: what brings me comfort and why I sometimes feel like I need it so much.

I started observing myself in my mindful walks. How I perceive myself on them, what I pay attention to, and what feels comfortable.

I found myself carrying my film camera with me most of the time. This made me reflect on how I engage in cycles and repetitions that make me feel validated. This validation sometimes takes the form of photos that remind me that I have existed in places, with people, and had feelings. Sometimes I hold onto memories to move forward, even if they feel incomplete or inaccurate.

embodyingCycles.mp4

Finally, after reading McKenzie's book "Raving as a Practice" I reflected on how raving is an ongoing practice that shapes how I feel, think, and who I am over time. I always come back to it. It is a constant — like walking and film photography.

These repetitions — walking, photographing, raving — have become ways of making sense of myself. They offer comfort, structure, and reminders of who I am. I return to them again and again, not just out of habit, but because they help me feel present, real, and connected.

I created an intimate setting that resembles a home — a bedroom — to exhibit my pieces. My aim is for viewers to feel comfortable engaging with the work, since they originate in private memories, while also conveying that the space they are in is as intimate as the memories it holds.

While my memories can be altered by time or distance, they still shape how I move through the world and how I interact with myself and others. The cycle of remembering and projecting into the future, the nostalgia (of past experiences, but also of experiences I have never lived), and the feelings evoked by the format in which those photos are presented have been my focus of exploration for this project.

Processing — generative 3D type — 2nd year

Typographic Narratives is a three-dimensional virtual space built entirely out of letterforms using Processing.

The word "sky" in numerous languages laid flat into a plane you can walk through, with the word "star" flickering in front of it, mimicking the behaviour of the element it depicts. The project explores the idea of a narrative that can be understood before it is actually read. This is done through behaviour, colour, texture, position, and rhythm rather than language itself.

The piece is built using Processing's PEasyCamera library to shape the work as an environment rather than a fixed composition — somewhere the viewer can actually move around in. To populate it, I built a database of the words "sky" and "star" translated into as many languages as I could find, and used a font that would allow me to read many different characters.

Runway, cyanotype, Blender + 3D print — 3rd year

This project started by asking what "PR0CESS" means to me. I explored how I think, how creativity flows best for me, and which strategies worked best when developing an idea into a final piece.

I started by exploring the idea of the natural process of photosynthesis, and used as a starting point the purple sulfur bacteria that photosynthesises using light but creates sulfur instead of oxygen.

Using an iterative, prompt-driven methodology, I fed images of the bacteria into Runway to generate prompts, fed those prompts back in for new images, and looped through that cycle. Cyanotype stemmed the project towards light itself, further studying the shapes from the pictures with the AxiDraw. From there, the specific qualities that interested me the most from the results I was getting were collaged as organic 3D forms.

From there the work moved into Blender, then early 3D-printed prototypes on the MakerBot Replicator II, whose looser, stringier output ended up reading as more organic than the more precise ones from the Prusa i3 MK3S. The final piece was modeled in TinkerCAD and printed on the MakerBot and the Prusa to compare the results. The Prusa's cleaner finish revealed how much of the organic quality came from the printer, not the form.

For the exhibition, the prints sat around Petri dishes as if the bacteria were growing out of them, with a few more mounted along the side of the plinth like fungi on a tree trunk, extending that organic feeling throughout the space. I made use of light to play with the translucent quality of the prints' PLA.

Working through it, I came across the concepts of "information foraging"1 and "rhizomatic learning"2, which left me thinking about how creativity and research are shaped as much by technology and the internet as by the people around us — by experiencing and interpreting the world together.

Arduino, environmental sensors, LED matrix panel — data visualisation — 3rd year

Fluxus Tiling is about collecting and visualising data. Fluctuations of light, humidity and temperature in my bathroom were recorded for a week and visualised using LED panels simulating bathroom tiles.

The data comes from an Arduino with a DHT22 temperature and humidity sensor, an LDR (Light Dependent Resistor) and an ISL29125 sensor (for the light intensity of the general red, green, and blue spectra of visible light) logged to an SD card. After a few tests, a full week of data was collected on a CSV file. Processing was used to clean and tidy the file that was then used to visualise the data. Sadly, the RGB sensor had intermittent issues when recording, so it stayed out of the visualisation.

Two square LED matrix panels were used to visualise the data in the form of hydraulic tile patterns. Two circles - mirrored diagonally -, map temperature and humidity to both radius and colour. The white lines that form the squares are mapped to the intensity of light.

Dissertation — Design History and Theory — 4th year

This essay aims to frame my experience as a trans neurodivergent person within the rave scene.

Raving for me is not an individual practice – and my favourite way to rave is with my closest friends. For that reason, I chose to interview the people who make me feel safe when we rave, those "who need it" – as McKenzie Wark would say –, like me. I intend to draw from the socio-political background that raves emerge, beginning with the "free parties" – or illegal raves – where I got to discover a different way of being. The main sources for my analysis are: Hakim Bey's "T.A.Z.: The Temporary Autonomous Zone, Ontological Anarchy, Poetic Terrorism" which brings the context to understand from where raves emerge, McKenzie Wark's "Raving" as an inspiration to understand and explain my own experiences and Eudald Espluga's "No seas tú mismo" ("Don't be yourself") to help situate these experiences within the broader political context where they take place. I will expose the binary structures through which we frame our identities and communities, while drawing on anti-punitive approaches to uncover the complex realities that such frameworks often obscure.
